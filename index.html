00 · Cold open

Would you buy something you expect to lose money on?

Hold that answer. We'll come back to it once we have the tools.

Meet Stock A

You own Stock A. You expect it to beat cash by 7% a year, with 30% volatility.

That's 0.23 units of return for every unit of risk. Not bad.

Now short Stock B

Stock B is a similar company. You expect it to go up 7% a year. So shorting it should lose you money, on average.

Short 69 cents of B for every dollar of A. Expected return drops. But risk drops by half.

Return per unit of risk went up

Scale that hedged position back up to the same 30% risk and it earns more than A alone.

Adding a losing position made the portfolio better. By the end of tonight, that should feel obvious.

01 · Risk, return, correlation

Three numbers run the whole show.

Expected return. Volatility. And correlation, the only one that lets you cut risk without giving up return.

Two assets, perfectly correlated

Every mix of the two sits on a straight line. Risk is just the weighted average. Combining them buys you nothing.

Loosen the link

At a correlation of 0.3 the line bends left. The 50/50 mix now has less risk than the average of its parts.

Uncorrelated

At zero the bend gets dramatic. Some mixes are less risky than either asset on its own.

Perfectly opposite

At −1 one asset's losses exactly cancel the other's gains. There's a mix with zero risk.

Neither asset changed. Only how they move together. Keep this picture in mind: shorting is how you manufacture negative correlation.

02 · Diversification

The only free lunch in finance.

Some risk disappears just by owning more things. Some risk never does. The market only pays you for one of them.

One stock

A typical single stock swings about 36% a year. Most of that has nothing to do with the economy: a product flop, a lawsuit, a CEO leaving.

That's idiosyncratic risk, also called stock-specific or diversifiable risk.

Ten stocks

Company-specific surprises start cancelling out. Risk falls fast, and it costs you nothing in expected return.

Thirty stocks

Most of the benefit is already captured. The curve is flattening against a floor.

The floor: systematic risk

At 100 stocks you're left with systematic risk: recessions, rate shocks, panics. No amount of stock picking removes it.

Why it matters: since anyone can diversify idiosyncratic risk away for free, the market won't pay you a premium for holding it. You're only paid for systematic risk. That's the core of CAPM.

Show the math
Equal-weight, N stocks, β = 1: σp² = σm² + σε² / N σm = 16%, σε = 32%

03 · The efficient frontier

Where every rational portfolio lives.

Out of every possible mix, only a thin edge is worth holding. Then cash turns that edge into a single answer.

Eight sectors

Each dot is a sector, plotted by its risk and expected return. Hover for details.

Every possible mix

Here are 3,000 random long-only portfolios built from those sectors. Notice how many beat the individual sectors on risk.

The efficient frontier

The upper-left edge is the efficient frontier: the most return for each level of risk. Anything below it is leaving money on the table.

Add a risk-free asset

Draw a line from the cash rate that just touches the frontier. That's the capital market line, and the touch point is the tangency portfolio. In theory, it's the market portfolio.

Everyone holds the same risky portfolio

A conservative investor mixes the tangency portfolio with cash. An aggressive one borrows to lever it up. Both are on the line, and both beat the frontier.

Move the cash rate and watch the tangency point shift. In practice, institutions deviate from the market portfolio on purpose. That deviation is active management.

04 · Risk-adjusted returns

Beta is cheap. Alpha is expensive.

Every return splits into what the market gave you and what skill added. Investors should only pay up for the second part.

An index fund

Each dot is one month: market return across, fund return up. The slope is beta. Where the line crosses zero is alpha.

An index fund is pure beta. It costs about 0.03% a year.

A closet indexer

Beta near 1, a sliver of alpha, more noise. If this fund charges hedge fund fees, you're paying 2-and-20 for something you could buy for 3 basis points.

Leveraged beta

Great returns in a bull market, but no skill: beta of 1.5, alpha of zero. The Sharpe ratio looks fine. The Treynor ratio and Jensen's alpha catch it.

Market neutral

Flat line, positive intercept. Returns barely depend on the market. This is what a hedge fund should be selling you: alpha with the beta stripped out.

The information ratio (alpha ÷ the noise around it) is how allocators score this kind of manager.

05 · Allocation vs. selection

Did you pick the right rooms, or the right furniture?

Brinson attribution splits a manager's outperformance into where they put the money and what they bought once they got there.

The pure allocator

This manager owns exactly the benchmark's stocks in each sector, but overweights Tech and underweights Energy. All the excess return is an allocation effect.

The pure stock picker

Same sector weights as the benchmark, but better stocks inside each sector. All the excess return is a selection effect.

How institutions use this

Pensions and endowments mostly make allocation decisions. Brinson, Hood and Beebower (1986) found allocation policy explained most of the variation over time in pension returns. (It's often misquoted as "90% of returns".)

They hire managers to deliver selection. A hedged equity fund is a pure selection bet: the allocation to the market has been stripped out.

06 · The institutional problem

60/40 isn't as diversified as it looks.

The classic portfolio has two asset classes, but only one of them really drives the risk. And sometimes the other one doesn't help.

Dollars aren't risk

60% of the money is in stocks. But stocks are so much more volatile than bonds that they drive about 94% of the portfolio's risk.

A 60/40 portfolio is really an equity bet with a cushion.

When bonds work: 2008

Stocks fell 37%. Bonds rose 5%. The cushion did its job.

When they don't: 2022

Inflation hit both at once. The S&P 500 fell 18% and the bond index fell 13%. A 60/40 portfolio lost about 16%.

The diversifier stopped diversifying. Institutions want return streams that don't depend on the stock market going up or on bonds behaving.

07 · The bridge

Shorting flips the sign.

We keep looking for assets with low correlation. The easiest way to get one is to take something you already understand and short it.

A stock, long

Each dot is a month. This stock has a beta of about 1.2 and moves with the market.

The same stock, short

A short position earns the negative of the stock's return. Every dot flips. Beta becomes −1.2, and the correlation flips sign too.

You just created a negatively correlated asset out of thin air.

The catch

On its own, a short has a negative expected return, because stocks drift up over time. You also pay to borrow the shares, and losses are theoretically unlimited (remember GameStop in January 2021).

So the short isn't the product. The spread between a long and a short is.

Pair it

Hold $100 of a stock you like (beta 1.3) and short a stock you don't (beta 1.0). Slide the short size.

At $130 short, the market exposure is gone. What's left is the gap between your two picks: your alpha.

Back to the paradox

This is the cold open. The short loses money on its own, but it cancels risk you weren't being paid for. That's why adding it raises return per unit of risk.

Hedge funds take this idea and run it across hundreds of positions.

08 · Equity hedge strategies

One dial: how much market do you keep?

Long-only, 130/30, long/short, market neutral, short-biased. They're not five different things. They're five settings on the same dial.

Long-only

100% long, nothing short. Gross exposure (long + short) is 100%, and so is net (long − short). Your result is mostly the market.

130/30

Short 30% and use the cash to buy 30% more longs. Net is still 100%, so it behaves like the market, but gross is 160%. You have more room to express views, including negative ones.

Long-biased long/short

The classic equity hedge fund: 120% long, 60% short. Net around 60%. You keep some market upside, cushion the downside, and earn alpha on both sides.

Try the market scenarios on the right.

Equity market neutral

Size the book so beta-adjusted net is zero. Long 100% at beta 1.1, short 110% at beta 1.0. The market can crash and the P&L barely moves. Returns are almost entirely alpha.

Quant shops go further and neutralize sectors and factors like value, size and momentum.

Short-biased

More short than long. These funds make money when the market falls, and they're rare because they fight the market's long-term upward drift.

Dollar-neutral isn't market-neutral

A pairs trade: long $10M of Booking, short $10M of Expedia. If Expedia has the higher beta, a sector rally hurts you even when your call is right.

Short only enough Expedia to match betas and the line goes flat. You earn the spread whatever the sector does.

09 · The allocator's view

You're on the investment committee now.

Two questions decide whether a hedge fund earns a spot: what's left after fees, and what it does to the whole portfolio.

Classic 2-and-20

A market-neutral fund earns cash plus 6% of gross alpha. After a 2% management fee and 20% of profits, how much of that alpha reaches you?

Less than half.

Today's fees

Fees have compressed. Average terms are closer to 1.5 and 17 now, but the fee drag on alpha is still large. Allocators negotiate hard, and the best managers don't have to.

Average fee levels vary by source and year; check a current industry survey before quoting.

Add a sleeve to 60/40

Take the net-of-fee fund (about 6% return, 6% volatility, almost no correlation to stocks) and carve a sleeve out of the 60/40.

Drag the sleeve size. The portfolio moves up and to the left: better return per unit of risk.

In a 2022-style regime

When stocks and bonds become positively correlated, the 60/40 gets riskier, and the uncorrelated sleeve matters even more.

The questions an allocator asks: Am I paying for alpha or beta? What's the correlation to what I already own? What are the lockups and gates?

10 · Wrap-up

You only get paid for risk you can't diversify away. So the most valuable thing you can add to a portfolio is a return stream that doesn't move with the one you already own. Equity hedge funds are a way to buy exactly that.
