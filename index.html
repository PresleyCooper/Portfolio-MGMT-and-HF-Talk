Risk, Return and Correlation

A portfolio's risk depends on each asset's volatility and on how the assets move together. Correlation is the input that can reduce risk without reducing expected return.

Correlation of +1

Every mix of the two assets lies on a straight line. Portfolio volatility equals the weighted average of the two volatilities, so combining them provides no diversification.

Correlation of 0.3

The curve bends to the left. A 50/50 mix has lower volatility than the weighted average of its components.

Correlation of 0

Some mixes have lower volatility than either asset on its own.

Correlation of −1

The assets' movements offset exactly, and one mix has zero volatility.

The individual assets are unchanged in every case. Only the correlation between them differs. A short position is one way to create negative correlation.

Formula
σp² = w²σA² + (1−w)²σB² + 2w(1−w)·ρ·σA·σB

Diversification: Systematic and Idiosyncratic Risk

Total risk has two components. Idiosyncratic risk falls as holdings are added. Systematic risk remains regardless of how many securities are held.

One stock

A single stock in this model has volatility of about 36% a year. Most of it comes from company-specific events such as earnings surprises, litigation or management changes.

This is idiosyncratic risk, also called stock-specific, unsystematic or diversifiable risk.

Ten stocks

Company-specific shocks partially offset one another. Volatility falls sharply with no reduction in expected return.

Thirty stocks

Most of the diversification benefit has been captured. Further additions reduce volatility only marginally.

Systematic risk

At 100 stocks the portfolio holds almost only systematic risk: exposure to recessions, interest rate changes and market-wide shocks. Security selection cannot remove it.

Because idiosyncratic risk can be eliminated at no cost, investors are not compensated for bearing it. Under CAPM, expected return is a function of systematic risk only.

Formula
Equal-weight, N stocks, β = 1: σp² = σm² + σε² / N σm = 16%, σε = 32%

The Efficient Frontier and the Market Portfolio

The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios with the highest expected return for each level of risk. Adding a risk-free asset identifies a single optimal risky portfolio.

Individual sectors

Each point is a sector, plotted by volatility and expected return.

Feasible portfolios

3,000 random long-only portfolios built from the eight sectors. Many have lower volatility than any single sector.

The efficient frontier

The upper-left boundary of the feasible set is the efficient frontier: the maximum expected return at each level of risk. Portfolios below it are inefficient.

The capital market line

The line from the risk-free rate tangent to the frontier is the capital market line. The tangency point is the risky portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio. Under CAPM assumptions, it is the market portfolio.

Two-fund separation

Investors combine the tangency portfolio with cash to reduce risk, or borrow to increase it. Every point on the line dominates the frontier below it.

A higher risk-free rate moves the tangency point toward riskier portfolios. In practice, institutions deviate from the market portfolio deliberately. That deviation is active management.

04

Risk-Adjusted Returns: Alpha and Beta

A fund's return can be decomposed into market exposure (beta) and return independent of the market (alpha). Beta is available at index-fund cost. Alpha is scarce.

Index fund

Each point is one month: market return on the horizontal axis, fund return on the vertical. The slope of the regression line is beta. The intercept is alpha.

An index fund is pure beta, available for roughly 0.03% a year.

Closet indexer

Beta near 1, minimal alpha, and additional tracking noise. At hedge fund fee levels, an investor would pay active fees for exposure available almost for free.

Leveraged beta

Beta of 1.5 and zero alpha. Returns are high in rising markets, but they come entirely from market exposure. The Sharpe ratio does not distinguish this from skill. The Treynor ratio and Jensen's alpha do.

Market neutral

A flat regression line with a positive intercept. Returns have little dependence on the market, and most of the return is alpha.

The information ratio, alpha divided by residual volatility, is the standard measure for this type of manager.

Formulas
Rfund − rf = α + β(Rm − rf) + ε Sharpe = (R − rf) / σ Treynor = (R − rf) / β Info ratio = α / σε

Allocation and Selection Effects

Brinson attribution separates a manager's excess return into the effect of sector weighting decisions and the effect of security selection within sectors.

Allocation only

The portfolio holds the benchmark's securities within each sector but overweights Tech and underweights Energy. All excess return is an allocation effect.

Selection only

Sector weights match the benchmark, but the securities chosen within each sector outperform. All excess return is a selection effect.

Institutional application

Pensions and endowments primarily make allocation decisions. Brinson, Hood and Beebower (1986) found that allocation policy explained most of the variation over time in pension fund returns. The finding is often misstated as explaining "90% of returns".

Institutions hire external managers for selection. A hedged equity fund isolates selection, because its market exposure has been removed.

Formulas
Allocation = (wp − wb)(rb − Rb) Selection = wb(rp − rb) Interaction = (wp − wb)(rp − rb)

Risk Concentration in a 60/40 Portfolio

A 60/40 portfolio holds two asset classes, but equities account for nearly all of its risk. The diversification benefit of bonds also depends on the stock–bond correlation, which is not stable.

Capital allocation vs. risk allocation

Stocks are 60% of the capital. Because stock volatility is much higher than bond volatility, stocks contribute about 94% of portfolio variance.

2008

The S&P 500 returned −37.0%. The Bloomberg US Aggregate returned +5.2%. Bonds offset part of the equity loss.

2022

Rising inflation and interest rates drove both lower. The S&P 500 returned −18.1% and the Aggregate −13.0%. A 60/40 portfolio returned about −16%.

Institutions therefore seek return streams that depend on neither equity market direction nor a stable stock–bond correlation.

07

Short Positions Invert Correlation

A short position returns the negative of the underlying security's return. Its beta and its correlation to the market have the opposite sign of the long position's.

Long position

Each point is one month. The stock has a beta of 1.2 and a correlation of about 0.7 to the market.

Short position

Every return changes sign. Beta becomes −1.2 and correlation becomes about −0.7.

Costs of shorting

A standalone short has a negative expected return, because equities earn a positive risk premium over time. Short sellers also pay a borrow fee and face theoretically unlimited losses and short squeezes, as in GameStop in January 2021.

The return source in a long/short strategy is therefore the spread between the long and short positions, not the short on its own.

A hedged pair

Long $100 of stock A (beta 1.3), short stock B (beta 1.0).

At $130 short, beta-adjusted net exposure is zero. The remaining return is the performance difference between the two stocks: alpha.

A Position with Negative Expected Return Can Improve a Portfolio

Shorting a security that is expected to rise lowers expected return. If it removes enough unrewarded risk, return per unit of risk still increases.

Stock A

Stock A has an expected return of 7% over cash and 30% volatility: a Sharpe ratio of 0.23.

Add a short in Stock B

Stock B is a similar company with a 0.9 correlation to A and an expected return of 7%. A short position in B therefore has a negative expected return.

Shorting $0.69 of B per $1 of A lowers expected excess return to 4.2% and volatility to 15.2%.

Higher Sharpe ratio

The Sharpe ratio rises from 0.23 to 0.28. Levered to A's 30% volatility, the hedged position has an expected excess return of 8.4%, compared with 7.0% for A alone.

The short loses money on its own but removes risk that was not being compensated. Equity long/short funds apply this across many positions.

Equity Hedge Strategies by Net Exposure

Long-only, 130/30, long/short, market neutral and short-biased strategies differ mainly in how much market exposure they retain.

Long-only

100% long, no short positions. Gross exposure (long + short) and net exposure (long − short) are both 100%. Returns are driven mostly by the market.

130/30

30% short, with the proceeds funding an additional 30% of longs. Net exposure remains 100% and gross exposure is 160%. The manager can express negative views as well as positive ones.

Long-biased long/short

The most common equity hedge structure: for example, 120% long and 60% short, for net exposure of about 60%. The fund retains some market exposure, reduces downside, and can earn alpha on both sides of the book.

Equity market neutral

Position sizes are set so that beta-adjusted net exposure is zero: 100% long at beta 1.1 and 110% short at beta 1.0. Market moves have little effect on P&L, and returns are mostly alpha.

Quantitative managers typically also neutralize sector and factor exposures such as value, size and momentum.

Short-biased

Short exposure exceeds long exposure, so the fund profits when the market falls. These funds are uncommon because they work against the long-run upward drift of equity markets.

Dollar-neutral vs. beta-neutral

Pairs trade: long $10M of Booking, short $10M of Expedia. If Expedia has the higher beta, a sector rally produces a loss even when Booking outperforms.

Sizing the Expedia short to match betas removes sector exposure. The P&L is then the spread between the two stocks in every scenario.

Betas and alphas are illustrative, not estimates for the actual companies.

Hedge Funds in an Institutional Portfolio

An allocation decision depends on net-of-fee alpha and on the fund's effect on total portfolio risk.

2 and 20

A market-neutral fund earns the cash rate plus 6% gross alpha. After a 2% management fee and a 20% performance fee, the investor retains about 43% of the alpha.

Lower fee structures

Average fees have declined from 2 and 20. At 1.5 and 17, the investor retains about 54% of the alpha. Fees remain a large share of gross alpha.

Average fee levels vary by source and year.

Adding a sleeve to 60/40

A net-of-fee market-neutral fund with about 6% expected return, 6% volatility and low correlation to equities is funded proportionally from the 60/40 portfolio.

A 20% allocation raises the Sharpe ratio and reduces the equity share of portfolio risk.

Positive stock–bond correlation

When the stock–bond correlation is positive, as in 2022, the 60/40 portfolio is riskier and the low-correlation sleeve has a larger effect.

Key allocator questions: Is the fund delivering alpha or beta? What is its correlation to existing holdings? What are the lockups, gates and redemption terms?

11 · Summary

Investors are compensated only for systematic risk. The most valuable addition to a portfolio is a return stream with low correlation to existing holdings. Equity long/short and market-neutral funds provide that exposure.
