01

Risk, Return and Correlation

A portfolio's risk depends on each asset's volatility and on how the assets move together. Correlation is the input that can reduce risk without reducing expected return.

Correlation of +1

Correlation ranges from −1 to +1. At +1 the two assets move in lockstep, so there is no diversification effect.

Every mix of the two lies on a straight line between them. Portfolio volatility is simply the weighted average of the two volatilities.

Correlation of 0.3

Lower correlation bends the curve to the left. Left is better: the same expected return now comes with lower risk.

For the 50/50 mix, volatility falls by 2.6 percentage points versus the weighted average.

Correlation of 0

At zero correlation the 50/50 mix carries 3.8 points less volatility than the weighted average, and some mixes are less risky than either asset alone.

This curve is the two-asset version of the efficient frontier.

Correlations in practice

Low correlation is powerful but hard to find. Public equities share exposure to the market, so they tend to be highly correlated with one another.

Even defensive sectors such as utilities remain positively correlated with technology and with the broad market.

Why alternatives matter

Many alternative investments have historically had low correlation to public stocks and bonds. Their value to a portfolio comes mainly from that low correlation, not from their standalone returns.

An investor seeking only high returns could hold public or private equity. Alternatives earn their place with moderate-to-high returns at low correlation. Most are still modestly positive, and few are reliably negative.

Creating negative correlation

Assets with reliably negative correlation are scarce, so investors create them. Shorting a stock inverts its correlation: a stock with +0.8 correlation to the market becomes −0.8 when held short.

Because most public equities are highly positively correlated, short positions in them are highly negatively correlated with a long equity portfolio.

Correlation of −1

At −1 the assets offset exactly, and one mix removes all volatility.

The extreme case is a long and a short in the same stock: correlation −1, zero volatility, and zero return, because the two positions cancel. Useful hedges short a different stock, as later sections show.

Formula
σp² = w²σA² + (1−w)²σB² + 2w(1−w)·ρ·σA·σB

Diversification: Systematic and Idiosyncratic Risk

Total risk has two components. Idiosyncratic risk falls as holdings are added. Systematic risk remains regardless of how many securities are held.

One stock

A single stock in this model has volatility of about 36% a year. Much of it comes from company-specific events: the loss of key management, a competitor taking share, product failures, or environmental and accounting scandals.

This is idiosyncratic risk, also called non-systematic, stock-specific or diversifiable risk.

Ten stocks

Spreading capital across ten companies brings volatility down to about 18.9%. Company-specific shocks partially offset one another, with no reduction in expected return.

Thirty stocks

Volatility falls to about 17%. Most of the diversification benefit has been captured.

Systematic risk

At 100 stocks almost all idiosyncratic risk is gone. What remains is systematic risk, the risk inherent in the market as a whole. It acts as a floor: any long-only portfolio, large or small, remains exposed to it.

Because idiosyncratic risk can be eliminated at almost no cost, investors are not compensated for bearing it. Under CAPM, expected return is a function of systematic risk only.

Measuring the two

Beta measures systematic risk and is the risk measure in CAPM. Standard deviation measures total risk, systematic plus idiosyncratic.

The Sharpe ratio scales return by total risk. The Treynor ratio scales return by beta only. For a well-diversified portfolio, systematic risk is the risk that matters.

Formula
Equal-weight, N stocks, β = 1: σp² = σm² + σε² / N σm = 16%, σε = 32%

The Efficient Frontier and the Market Portfolio

The efficient frontier is the set of portfolios with the highest expected return for each level of risk. Adding a risk-free asset identifies a single optimal risky portfolio.

Individual sectors

Each point is a sector, plotted by volatility and expected return. Technology has the highest expected return and volatility. Utilities and consumer staples have the lowest.

Combining sectors

3,000 random long-only portfolios built from the eight sectors. Many combinations have lower volatility and higher expected return than any single sector.

Real estate, for example, has an expected return of 7.5%. At real estate's 24.6% volatility, a diversified portfolio can earn about 11.2%. Alternatively, it can earn the same 7.5% at about 12.7% volatility, roughly half the risk.

The efficient frontier

The upper-left boundary of these combinations is the efficient frontier: the maximum expected return at each level of risk.

The lower edge of the feasible set is not efficient. For the same risk, a portfolio above it always earns more.

The capital market line

Adding a risk-free asset, here at 3%, allows a line from the risk-free rate that just touches the frontier: the capital market line.

The tangency point is the risky portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio. Under CAPM assumptions it is the market portfolio.

Choosing a point on the line

A conservative investor holds a mix such as 50% risk-free asset and 50% market portfolio. An investor seeking more return borrows and levers the market portfolio.

Every point on the line has the same, maximum Sharpe ratio; investors choose only how much risk to take. Borrowing above the risk-free rate makes the line slightly flatter beyond the tangency point.

Risk-Adjusted Returns: Alpha and Beta

A fund's return can be decomposed into market exposure (beta) and return independent of the market (alpha). Beta is available at index-fund cost. Alpha is scarce.

Index fund

Market return is on the horizontal axis and fund return on the vertical. The slope of the regression line is beta. The intercept is alpha.

An index fund such as VOO or SPY tracks the market with a beta of 1 and zero alpha. Pure beta is available for about 0.03% a year, so investors should not pay active fees for it.

Closet indexer

A closet indexer charges active fees but tracks the benchmark closely, with a beta near 0.95 and minimal alpha. Fear of tracking error keeps it from deviating enough to generate meaningful alpha.

At hedge fund fee levels, the investor's net return would be at or below the market's. Generating alpha requires deviating from the market.

Leveraged beta

A beta of 1.5 and zero alpha. Returns are high in rising markets, but they come entirely from market exposure.

The Sharpe ratio is the same 0.37 as the index fund, so it does not distinguish leverage from skill. Jensen's alpha (0%) and the Treynor ratio do.

Market neutral

A beta near zero, so the return comes almost entirely from alpha. Because it barely moves with the market, a market-neutral fund is a strong diversifier inside a broader portfolio.

The information ratio, alpha divided by residual volatility, is the standard measure for this type of manager.

Formulas
Rfund − rf = α + β(Rm − rf) + ε Sharpe = (R − rf) / σ Treynor = (R − rf) / β Info ratio = α / σε

Allocation and Selection Effects

Brinson attribution separates a manager's excess return into the effect of sector weighting decisions and the effect of security selection within sectors.

Allocation effect

The manager holds each sector exactly as the benchmark does, for example through sector ETFs, but changes the sector weights: overweight Tech in a strong tech market and underweight Energy during its decline. All excess return is an allocation effect.

Allocation can add significant return, but it concentrates risk in a few sector calls.

Selection effect

Sector weights match the benchmark exactly. Excess return comes from choosing companies that outperform the average company in their sector. All excess return is a selection effect.

Both effects

Most active managers produce some of each, plus an interaction term from overweighting the sectors where their picks also outperform.

Formulas
Allocation = (wp − wb)(rb − Rb) Selection = wb(rp − rb) Interaction = (wp − wb)(rp − rb)

Institutional practice

Institutions generally keep their own allocation close to the benchmark and avoid large allocation bets. They hire external managers to generate selection alpha.

Managers with opposite style tilts are often combined. Pairing a growth manager with a value manager leaves the total portfolio with little net style bias, while each manager still contributes stock-selection alpha.

Risk Concentration in a 60/40 Portfolio

A 60/40 portfolio holds two asset classes, but equities account for nearly all of its risk. The diversification benefit of bonds also depends on the stock–bond correlation, which is not stable.

Capital allocation vs. risk allocation

Stocks are 60% of the capital, but because stock volatility is much higher than bond volatility, stocks contribute about 94% of portfolio variance. The dollar weights say little about the risk weights.

Bonds act mainly as a dampener on equity moves.

2008

The S&P 500 returned −37.0%. The Bloomberg US Aggregate returned +5.2%. Bonds offset part of the equity loss.

2022

Rising inflation and interest rates drove both lower. The S&P 500 returned −18.1% and the Aggregate −13.0%, so bonds provided no dampening. A 60/40 portfolio returned about −16%.

Institutions therefore seek return streams that depend on neither equity market direction nor a stable stock–bond correlation.

Short Positions Invert Correlation

A short position returns the negative of the underlying security's return. Its beta and its correlation to the market have the opposite sign of the long position's.

Long position

Short positions create negative correlation with the rest of a portfolio, or with its equity holdings specifically.

Each point is one month. This stock has a beta of 1.2 and a correlation of about 0.7 to the market.

Short position

Every return changes sign. Beta becomes −1.2 and correlation becomes about −0.7.

Negative expected return

A standalone short has a negative expected return. Investors are paid a positive risk premium for bearing systematic risk, so a long equity position has a positive expected return and a short has a negative one.

Short sellers also pay a borrow fee on the shares.

Asymmetric payoff

A long position can gain without limit but can lose at most 100%. A short is the reverse: it can gain at most 100% and its losses are theoretically unlimited.

Short squeezes make that risk concrete, as in GameStop in January 2021.

Pairs trading

In a long/short strategy the return source is the spread between the long and short positions, not the short on its own.

Long $100 of stock A (beta 1.3), short stock B (beta 1.0). At $130 short, beta-adjusted exposure is zero, and the remaining return is the performance difference between the two: alpha. The bet is that A outperforms B in both rising and falling markets.

Common pairs: Coca-Cola and PepsiCo, Booking and Expedia, Union Pacific with CSX or Norfolk Southern.

A Position with Negative Expected Return Can Improve a Portfolio

Shorting a security that is expected to rise lowers expected return. If it removes enough unrewarded risk, return per unit of risk still increases.

Stock A

Stock A has an expected return of 7% over cash and 30% volatility: a Sharpe ratio of 0.23.

Add a short in Stock B

Stock B is a similar company with a 0.9 correlation to A and an expected return of 7%. A short position in B therefore has a negative expected return.

Shorting $0.69 of B per $1 of A lowers expected excess return to 4.2%, but cuts volatility from 30% to 15.2%.

Higher return per unit of risk

The Sharpe ratio rises from 0.23 to 0.28. This is the diversification effect from the correlation section.

Scaled up to A's original 30% volatility, the hedged position has an expected excess return of 8.4%, compared with 7.0% for A alone.

Equity Hedge Strategies by Net Exposure

Long-only, 130/30, long/short, market neutral and short-biased strategies differ mainly in how much market exposure they retain.

Long-only

100% long, no short positions. Gross exposure (long + short) and net exposure (long − short) are both 100%. Returns are driven mostly by the market.

130/30

30% short, with the short proceeds funding an additional 30% of longs. Net exposure remains 100% and gross exposure is 160%. The manager can express negative views as well as positive ones.

Long-biased long/short

The most common long/short structure, for example 120% long and 60% short. Because equities rise over time, these funds keep some positive beta while hedging part of their long exposure.

Equity market neutral

Beta-adjusted net exposure is zero, so the market has little effect on P&L. Returns come only from the spread between longs and shorts: alpha. Here, 100% long at beta 1.1 and 110% short at beta 1.0.

Managers typically also neutralize sector and factor exposures such as value, size and momentum, so returns depend almost entirely on skill. These funds are less common than long-biased funds.

Short-biased

Short exposure exceeds long exposure, so the fund profits when the market falls. These funds are rare because they work against the long-run upward drift of equity markets.

Dollar-neutral vs. beta-neutral

$10M long Booking and $10M short Expedia is dollar-neutral but not market-neutral if their betas differ. With Booking at 0.75 and Expedia at 1.5, a sector rally produces a loss even when Booking outperforms.

Positions should be weighted by beta, not dollars. Shorting $5M of Expedia matches the betas, and the P&L is the spread between the two stocks in every scenario.

Betas and alphas are illustrative, not estimates for the actual companies.

Hedge Funds in an Institutional Portfolio

An allocation decision depends on net-of-fee alpha and on the fund's effect on total portfolio risk.

2 and 20

A market-neutral fund earns the cash rate plus 6% gross alpha. After a 2% management fee and a 20% performance fee, the investor retains about 43% of the alpha.

Lower fee structures

Average fees have declined from 2 and 20. At 1.5 and 17, the investor retains about 54% of the alpha. Fees remain a large share of gross alpha.

Average fee levels vary by source and year.

Adding a sleeve to 60/40

A net-of-fee market-neutral fund with about 6% expected return, 6% volatility and low correlation to equities is funded proportionally from the 60/40 portfolio.

A 20% allocation raises the Sharpe ratio and reduces the equity share of portfolio risk.

Positive stock–bond correlation

When the stock–bond correlation is positive, as in 2022, the 60/40 portfolio is riskier and the low-correlation sleeve has a larger effect.

Key allocator questions: Is the fund delivering alpha or beta? What is its correlation to existing holdings? What are the lockups, gates and redemption terms?

11 · Summary

Investors are compensated only for systematic risk. The most valuable addition to a portfolio is a return stream with low correlation to existing holdings. Equity long/short and market-neutral funds provide that exposure.
