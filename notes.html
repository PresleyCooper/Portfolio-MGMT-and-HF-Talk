Opening 1 min

  • General portfolio management concepts come first. They explain why equity hedge strategies matter.
  • Color key used throughout: blue is market/beta, green is long, orange is short, gold is alpha.

01 · Risk, return and correlation 6 min

  • A portfolio's risk depends on each asset's volatility and on how the assets move together. Correlation can reduce risk without reducing expected return.
  • Correlation runs from −1 to +1. At +1 there is no diversification: every mix sits on the straight line.
  • 0.3: the curve bends left, which is good because it means the same return at lower risk. 2.6 points of risk removed. At 0: 3.8 points removed. This curve is the two-asset efficient frontier.
  • Correlations in practice: public equities mostly move together because they share market exposure. Even tech vs. utilities is positive.
  • Alternatives: their value is low correlation more than high returns. For high returns alone you would just hold public or private equity. Most alternatives are still somewhat positive.
  • Negative correlation is hard to find, so we manufacture it. Short Microsoft: +0.8 becomes −0.8.
  • At −1, risk can be removed entirely. But long and short the same stock gives zero risk and zero return. The positions cancel.

02 · Systematic and idiosyncratic risk 5 min

  • Single stock: management loss, a competitor, product failures, scandals. That is idiosyncratic, or non-systematic, risk.
  • 10 stocks: about 18.9%. 30 stocks: about 17%. 100 stocks: nearly all idiosyncratic risk is gone.
  • The floor is systematic risk, the risk of the market as a whole. Any long-only portfolio carries it.
  • Idiosyncratic risk can be diversified away at almost no cost, so investors aren't compensated for it. Under CAPM, expected return depends on beta only.
  • Beta measures systematic risk; standard deviation measures total risk. Sharpe uses total risk, Treynor uses beta.

03 · Efficient frontier and market portfolio 6 min

  • Tech has the highest return and volatility; utilities and staples the lowest. Sector numbers are illustrative.
  • Real estate: 7.5% return at 24.6% volatility. At that same risk a diversified portfolio earns about 11.2%. Or it can earn 7.5% at about 12.7% volatility.
  • The upper-left edge is the efficient frontier. The lower edge isn't used, because you can always move up to a better portfolio at the same risk.
  • Add a 3% risk-free asset and draw the line tangent to the frontier. The tangency point is the market portfolio, with the highest Sharpe ratio.
  • Conservative: for example 50% risk-free and 50% market portfolio. Aggressive: borrow (at or slightly above the risk-free rate) and lever up.

04 · Alpha and beta 5 min

  • Beta is the slope; alpha is the intercept.
  • Index fund (VOO, SPY): beta 1, alpha 0, about 3 basis points a year. Nobody should pay a manager for beta.
  • Closet indexers: beta around 0.95, little alpha, afraid of tracking error. With hedge fund fees the investor ends up at or below the market.
  • Leveraged beta: the Sharpe ratio is still 0.37, the same as the index. Jensen's alpha (0%) and Treynor reveal there's no skill.
  • Market neutral: beta near zero, return from alpha alone. That makes it a strong diversifier, covered later.

05 · Allocation and selection 4 min

  • Reading the box chart: width is weight, height is the sector's return minus the benchmark total. Blue strip is allocation, gold is selection, gray corner is interaction.
  • Allocation: picking the right sectors, for example via sector ETFs. Overweight tech 30% to 40% while it beats the benchmark: +0.81% from tech, +2.20% across sectors.
  • Selection: same weights as the benchmark, better companies within each sector. Tech picks return 21% vs. 18%: +0.90% from tech, +1.85% total.
  • The waterfall adds up all sectors: 9.9% benchmark to 14.1% portfolio. Click the sector buttons to show any sector live.
  • Institutions stay close to the benchmark on allocation and hire managers for selection.
  • They pair managers with opposite tilts, such as growth with value. The combined portfolio has little factor bias, but both managers still add selection alpha.

06 · The 60/40 portfolio 3 min

  • 60% of the dollars, about 94% of the risk. Dollar weights don't tell you risk weights.
  • Bonds are a dampener: 2008 S&P −37.0%, Agg +5.2%. In 2022 they weren't: S&P −18.1%, Agg −13.0%, 60/40 about −16%.
  • Institutions want returns that depend on neither equity direction nor a stable stock–bond correlation. That leads to short positions.

07 · Short positions 5 min

  • Shorts artificially create negative correlation with the portfolio. Beta 1.2 becomes −1.2, and correlation 0.7 becomes −0.7.
  • A standalone short has negative expected return: investors are paid for systematic risk, so shorting it means paying that premium. There is also a borrow fee.
  • Payoff asymmetry: a long can lose at most 100% with unlimited upside. A short gains at most 100% with unlimited losses (GameStop, January 2021).
  • The return source is the spread, not the short. Pairs trading: long $100 of A, short $130 of B, beta-adjusted exposure zero. The bet is that A beats B in up and down markets.
  • Examples: Coke/Pepsi, Booking/Expedia, Union Pacific with CSX or Norfolk Southern.

08 · Negative expected return, better portfolio 2 min

  • Stock A: 7% at 30% volatility. Adding a short in B lowers return to 4.2% but volatility to 15.2%.
  • Return per unit of risk rises from 0.23 to 0.28. That is the diversification effect from section 01.
  • Scaled back up to 30% volatility, it earns 8.4% vs. 7.0% for A alone.

09 · Equity strategies by net exposure 6 min

  • Long-only: 100% net, returns driven mostly by the market.
  • 130/30: 30% short funds 30% more longs. Net 100%, gross 160%. The manager can express negative views as well as positive ones.
  • Long-biased long/short is the most common type. Equities rise over time, so these funds keep some positive beta but hedge part of it.
  • Market neutral: zero beta, return only from the spread. Managers usually also neutralize sector and factor exposures (value, size, momentum), leaving pure skill. Less common.
  • Short-biased: rare, because it fights the market's upward drift.
  • Dollar-neutral vs. beta-neutral: if Booking's beta is 0.75 and Expedia's is 1.5, then $10M/$10M is not market neutral. Weight by beta: short $5M of Expedia.

10 · Hedge funds in an institutional portfolio 3 min

  • Not covered in the recording. This is the "talk about it later" diversification payoff from market neutral.
  • 2 and 20 on 6% gross alpha leaves the investor about 43%. At 1.5 and 17, about 54%.
  • A 20% market-neutral sleeve in a 60/40 raises the Sharpe ratio and lowers the equity share of risk. The effect is bigger when stock–bond correlation is positive, as in 2022.
