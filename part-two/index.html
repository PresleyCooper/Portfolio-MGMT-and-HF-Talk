A Strategy That Rarely Loses

Part one showed why investors value return streams with low correlation to the stock market. Some hedge fund strategies deliver exactly that in normal markets, then fail at the same time as stocks.

Eleven years of steady gains

From 1997 through 2007, this hedge fund strategy index rose 156%. It made money in 80% of months, returned 8.9% a year, and had volatility of only 3.9%.

Its worst month in those eleven years was −3.2%. Would you invest?

Compared with stocks

Over the same period the US stock market rose 141%, with four times the volatility and a 45% drawdown in 2000–2002.

On a risk-adjusted basis, the strategy looked far better than equities, and its correlation to stocks was low.

September and October 2008

The strategy is convertible arbitrage. It lost 10.3% in September 2008 and 12.4% in October, and fell 29% from peak to trough, more than seven times its worst month of the prior decade.

The losses arrived in the same months as the stock market's worst losses.

Not an isolated case

Fixed income arbitrage and merger arbitrage show the same pattern: small, frequent gains and losses concentrated in market crises (1998, 2008 and March 2020).

The question for every strategy in this session: what risk is it being paid to bear, and when does that risk show up?

Payoff Shape: Selling or Buying Insurance

Correlation is a single number. It hides whether a strategy's link to the market is the same in good and bad months. Plotting monthly returns against the market shows the shape.

Merger arbitrage

Each dot is one month, 1997–2021. In rising markets, merger arbitrage barely moves with stocks (beta about 0.13). In falling markets its beta rises to about 0.21.

The fitted line is steeper on the left, where markets fall, than on the right. This is a concave payoff: it looks like the payoff of someone who sells insurance on the market.

Convertible arbitrage

The same shape, more pronounced: down-market beta of 0.27 against an up-market beta of 0.19, with the largest losses in the largest market declines.

Mitchell and Pulvino (2001) showed merger arbitrage returns resemble a short put option on the market. Several relative value strategies share that profile.

Trend following (CTAs)

Managed futures funds show the opposite shape. Correlation to stocks is about zero overall, but the down-market beta is negative (−0.23): these funds tended to gain in the worst stock market months.

The curve bends up on both sides. This is a convex payoff, like owning insurance: many small losses, occasional large gains.

Global macro

Global macro sits in between, with a modestly convex profile: lower beta in down markets (0.10) than in up markets (0.19).

Discretionary macro managers can position for crises, but they are not systematically long volatility.

The map for this session

The bars show down-market beta minus up-market beta for each strategy. Positive bars are concave: steady in calm markets, exposed in crises. Negative bars are convex.

Low average correlation is not the same as protection in a crash. Allocators look at both. The next two chapters take one example of each: merger arbitrage for concave and the 1992 pound trade for convex.

Merger Arbitrage

After an acquisition is announced, the target's stock usually trades below the offer price. The gap, or spread, is what the market charges for the risk that the deal fails.

Event-driven

Microsoft and Activision

On January 18, 2022, Microsoft agreed to buy Activision Blizzard for $95 a share in cash. Activision had closed at $65.39 the trading day before the announcement.

At the end of January Activision traded at $79.01: a gross spread of 20% for an investor who bought and waited for the deal to close.

The spread as a probability

If the deal fails, the stock likely falls back toward its pre-deal price. Comparing the gain if it closes with the loss if it breaks gives the market's implied probability of completion.

With a $65.39 fallback, the January price implied about a 46% chance of closing. Regulatory risk was the reason: the deal needed approval from the US, UK and EU.

Formula
Implied probability = (Price − Fallback) / (Offer − Fallback) (79.01 − 65.39) / (95 − 65.39) = 46%

Regulators move the spread

The FTC sued to block the deal in December 2022. In April 2023 the UK's Competition and Markets Authority blocked it, and the implied probability fell from 68% to 42% in a month.

In July 2023 a US judge denied the FTC's request for an injunction, and the stock jumped toward $95. The deal closed on October 13, 2023.

Twitter: the fallback moves too

Elon Musk agreed to buy Twitter at $54.20 in April 2022. By June the stock traded below its pre-deal price of $39.31, and on July 11, after Musk moved to terminate, it fell to $32.65.

The fallback is not fixed. Tech stocks fell sharply in 2022, so the price Twitter would trade at without a deal fell too. That market exposure is why merger arbitrage loses money in broad selloffs.

Kroger and Albertsons: a break

Kroger agreed to buy Albertsons in October 2022 for $34.10, reduced by a $6.85 special dividend to $27.25. Albertsons traded between about $18 and $23 for the next two years, a spread of roughly 18% to 50%.

The FTC sued in February 2024, and on December 10, 2024 federal and state judges blocked the deal. The wide spread had priced in a low chance of success.

The typical trade

Most deals are less dramatic. A target at $48 with a $50 offer and a $35 fallback offers a 4% gain if the deal closes and a 27% loss if it breaks. The market is implying an 87% chance of closing.

Arbitrageurs earn a small, frequent return for providing insurance to shareholders who want to sell now, and absorb occasional large losses. That is the concave shape from chapter 2.

Global Macro: Betting Against a Peg

Macro funds take positions in currencies, interest rates, equity indices and commodities based on views about central banks, inflation, growth and politics. The best-known macro trade is a bet against a currency peg.

Macro

The pound in the ERM

In October 1990 the UK joined the European Exchange Rate Mechanism at a central rate of 2.95 Deutsche marks per pound. The pound had to stay within a 6% band, between 2.778 and 3.132.

To defend the band, the Bank of England had to buy pounds or raise interest rates.

How a central bank props up its currency

An exchange rate is a price set by supply and demand. When more people sell pounds than buy them, the pound falls. A central bank can push back in two ways.

Buy poundsThe Bank of England spends its reserves of marks and dollars to buy pounds. That adds demand and soaks up the pounds others are selling, holding the price up.
Raise interest ratesPound deposits and gilts pay more, so investors want to hold pounds. Shorting gets expensive too: a short seller borrows pounds and pays the UK rate while waiting.

The limits: reserves run out, and high rates slow an economy that is already in recession. Speculators knew both, which is why they doubted the defense would last.

Pinned to the floor

By the summer of 1992 the UK was in recession, while the Bundesbank kept German rates high after reunification. Defending the pound required rates the UK economy could not easily bear.

From late August the pound traded just above the 2.778 floor.

An asymmetric bet

A short position in pounds near the floor had limited downside: if the peg held, the pound was unlikely to rise far. If the peg broke, the pound could fall a long way.

Macro managers look for exactly this kind of trade, where the potential gain is much larger than the potential loss. It is the convex shape from chapter 2: a small loss if nothing happens, a large gain if something breaks.

Black Wednesday

On September 16, 1992, the Bank of England raised its base rate from 10% to 12% and announced a further rise to 15%. That evening the UK suspended its ERM membership.

The pound closed at 2.6323 marks the next day, 5% below the floor.

The payoff

By October 5 the pound had fallen to 2.393, 14% below its September 16 level. George Soros's Quantum Fund was reported to have held a short position of about $10 billion and made about $1 billion.

Distressed Debt and Activism

Distressed investors buy the debt of companies in or near bankruptcy. The return depends on what the company is worth and where the investor sits in the capital structure.

Event-driven

A healthy capital structure

An illustrative company owes $400 million of senior secured loans, $300 million of senior unsecured bonds and $150 million of subordinated notes. At an enterprise value of $1.1 billion, every lender is covered and the equity is worth $250 million.

Distress

If the business deteriorates and enterprise value falls to $800 million, the equity is nearly worthless and the subordinated notes are only partly covered. Bond prices fall well below par.

The absolute priority rule

In bankruptcy, value is paid out in order of seniority. At $550 million, the secured loans recover in full, the unsecured bonds recover 50 cents on the dollar, and the subordinated notes and equity receive nothing.

The unsecured bonds are the fulcrum security: the most senior class that is not paid in full. Its holders typically receive the reorganized company's new equity.

The distressed trade

A distressed fund that buys the unsecured bonds at 30 cents and recovers 50 cents earns 67%. If the investor misjudges enterprise value, recovery can be far lower.

The skill is valuing the business and understanding the legal process: who controls the restructuring, and how value is negotiated among creditors.

Credit cycle exposure

Distressed returns depend on the credit cycle. The EDHEC distressed securities index lost 21% in 2008, when credit markets froze, and gained 30% in 2009 as defaulted debt recovered.

Activism

Activist funds buy a stake in a public company and push for change: board seats, cost cuts, buybacks, a spinoff or a sale. Returns depend on the change happening, so outcomes are event-driven.

In 2024, Trian lost a proxy fight for Disney board seats, while Elliott reached a settlement giving it board seats at Southwest Airlines.

One View, Many Trades: 2022

A macro view has to be expressed through specific positions. Two managers with the same view can earn very different returns depending on the instruments they choose.

Macro

The view at the start of 2022

US inflation reached 7.0% in December 2021, the highest in four decades, while the Federal Reserve's policy rate was still near zero.

The view: inflation would persist and the Fed would raise rates faster than markets expected. The Fed raised its target range from 0–0.25% to 4.25–4.50% by December 2022.

Expression: short Treasuries

Rising rates push bond prices down. The 10-year Treasury yield rose from about 1.5% to 3.6% (monthly averages), so a short position in 10-year Treasuries gained about 13%.

Expression: long dollar, short yen

The Bank of Japan kept its policy rate at −0.1% while the Fed hiked. The yield gap pushed the dollar up 14% against the yen, one of the largest annual moves in decades.

Right view, weak expression

Gold is often described as an inflation hedge. In 2022 it was roughly flat, because rising real interest rates offset inflation.

Oil rose 6% for the year, but it was up more than 40% by June before giving most of it back. Timing mattered as much as direction.

Building the book

Toggle each position long or short. A diversified macro book spreads one view across several markets so that no single expression decides the year.

The HFRI Macro Index gained 9.3% in 2022, when the S&P 500 lost 18.1%.

The Carry Trade

Borrow in a currency with low interest rates, invest in one with high rates, and earn the difference. The trade pays steadily until the funding currency suddenly strengthens.

Macro

No carry in 2021

In 2021 both the Fed and the Bank of Japan held rates near zero. Borrowing yen to hold dollars earned almost nothing.

The gap opens

From March 2022 the Fed raised rates to 5.25–5.50% while the Bank of Japan held at −0.1%. A dollar position funded in yen earned more than 5% a year in interest alone.

The yen also weakened, from 115 per dollar at the start of 2022 to 161.7 on July 10, 2024. Carry and currency gains added together.

August 2024

On July 31, 2024 the Bank of Japan raised rates to 0.25%. Two days later a weak US jobs report raised expectations of Fed cuts.

By August 5 the dollar had fallen to 143.95 yen, 11% below its July peak, and Japan's Nikkei index fell 12.4% in one day as leveraged carry positions were unwound.

Steady gains, sudden losses

Carry trades are often described as going "up the stairs and down the elevator." At 5x leverage, a position entered in early 2024 lost most of its gains in a few weeks.

Drag the entry date and leverage to see how quickly the payoff changes.

Trend Following

Commodity trading advisors (CTAs) follow rules: own markets that have been rising, short markets that have been falling, across stocks, bonds, currencies and commodities.

Managed futures

The rule

A simple version: each month, if a market is up over the past 12 months, hold it long; if it is down, hold it short.

In the S&P 500, the 12-month rule turned short in February 2008, stayed short through the crash, and turned long again in November 2009. From 2006 to 2010 the index fell 3% while the rule gained 47%.

Across eight markets

The same rule applied to the S&P 500, 10-year Treasuries, gold, oil and four currencies, with each position scaled to equal volatility.

Over time it earns modest returns with little relationship to the stock market.

Crisis alpha

In 2008 the eight-market rule gained strongly while the S&P 500 fell. It was short equities and long Treasuries by the time the worst losses arrived.

The EDHEC CTA index gained 15.6% in 2008.

The worst months for stocks

In the ten worst months for US stocks from 1997 to 2021, the CTA index was positive in eight. Merger arbitrage was negative in seven of them.

This is the convex profile from chapter 2, seen month by month.

2022 and the cost of insurance

In 2022, with stocks and bonds falling together, the HFRI 500 Systematic Directional index gained 16.5%. The simple eight-market version here gained less, because it lacks the commodity and interest rate futures that drove most CTA gains that year.

The cost is the stretches of flat or negative returns in choppy markets. The oil 2006–10 view shows a failure: the rule was still long when oil collapsed in late 2008 and short during the 2009 rebound. Change the lookback to see how results depend on the setting.

Relative Value and Leverage

Relative value funds buy one security and short a nearly identical one, betting that a small price gap closes. Small gaps require large leverage, and leverage determines whether the fund survives until the gap closes.

Relative value

A convergence trade

A fund buys a slightly cheaper bond and shorts a nearly identical, slightly more expensive one. The spread between them is a fraction of a percent, and it should close as the bonds mature.

Unlevered, the trade earns very little. At 25x leverage the return on capital becomes attractive.

The spread path in this chart is illustrative.

Spreads widen first

In a crisis, investors sell less liquid securities and buy the most liquid ones. Spreads that "should" converge widen instead.

At 25x leverage the losses trigger a margin call, and the position is closed at the widest spread, locking in the loss. At 5x the fund survives and profits as the spread converges.

Long-Term Capital Management

LTCM, founded in 1994 by Salomon Brothers bond traders and two future Nobel laureates, earned about 21%, 43%, 41% and 17% in its first four years.

At the start of 1998 it had $4.7 billion of capital, about $129 billion of assets (over 25 to 1 leverage) and derivatives with notional value of about $1.25 trillion.

August and September 1998

Russia defaulted on August 17, 1998. Credit and liquidity spreads widened worldwide, and LTCM lost $1.85 billion in August alone and $4.6 billion in less than four months.

On September 23 the New York Fed organized a $3.6 billion recapitalization by 14 banks to avoid a disorderly liquidation.

The same pattern in 2008 and 2020

Relative value indexes lost money in the same months: fixed income arbitrage fell 8.0% in October 1998 and 17.4% in 2008. Convertible arbitrage lost 26.5% in 2008.

In March 2020 the Treasury cash-futures basis trade, a large leveraged relative value position, unwound rapidly, and the Fed bought Treasuries in record amounts to restore market functioning.

Multi-Strategy Platforms

The largest hedge fund firms run hundreds of small trading teams, or pods, under central risk management. Firms such as Citadel, Millennium and Point72 have attracted a large share of new institutional capital.

Many small teams

Each pod runs a market-neutral book in one area: an equity sector, rates, credit or commodities. The platform hedges out market exposure centrally.

This simulation runs 40 pods for five years. Pods differ in skill: the average true Sharpe ratio is 0.35, and about a quarter have no edge at all. Their returns are nearly uncorrelated with one another.

Tight drawdown limits

A pod that loses more than a set amount, often around 5%, has its capital cut or is closed, and the capital goes to another team.

Cutting losers early limits losses from teams whose edge has disappeared and keeps the platform's return smooth.

Diversification across pods

Combining 40 pods with low correlation to one another produces a platform Sharpe ratio far higher than the average pod's. This is part one's diversification lesson applied to managers instead of stocks.

Raise the correlation between pods to see the benefit shrink.

The cost

Many platforms charge pass-through fees: investors pay the firm's costs, including trader compensation, in addition to a performance fee. Total costs can exceed the traditional 2 and 20.

Platforms are also exposed when many pods hold similar crowded trades. Citadel's largest funds lost about 55% in 2008, then gained 62% in 2009. Its flagship Wellington fund returned 15.1% in 2024.

The Allocator's View

An endowment or pension fund does not choose one strategy. It builds a hedge fund allocation alongside its stocks and bonds, and judges it by what it does to the whole portfolio.

The starting point: 60/40

The base portfolio is 60% US stocks and 40% 10-year Treasuries, rebalanced monthly, using real returns from 1997 to mid-2021.

Adding a hedge fund sleeve

A 20% sleeve, funded proportionally from stocks and bonds, split across merger arbitrage, relative value, global macro and trend following.

Use the sliders to change the mix. The readouts update for the full 1997–2021 period.

Correlation in calm and crisis months

The heatmap shows correlations in normal months and in months when stocks fell more than 5%. Concave strategies become more correlated with stocks and with one another in crises.

Trend following does the opposite. That is why allocators pair the two.

Stress test: 2008

In 2008, a sleeve of only concave strategies would have fallen alongside stocks. Adding trend following and macro offset part of the loss.

Try setting the sleeve to merger arbitrage and relative value only, then to trend following only.

2022: when bonds failed

The EDHEC series ends in mid-2021. In 2022, reported HFR index returns were: macro +9.3%, relative value −0.8%, event-driven −4.6% and equity hedge −10.2%, against −18.1% for the S&P 500 and −13.0% for US bonds.

The strategies that helped most were the ones that do not depend on stocks and bonds moving in opposite directions.

13 · Summary

Every hedge fund strategy is paid to take a specific risk off someone else's hands. The shape of the payoff shows which risk, and when it will hurt.

Merger arbitrageEarns the deal spread; loses when deals break and when markets sell off.
DistressedValues the business and the claim; tied to the credit cycle.
Global macroSeeks asymmetric bets on policy; expression matters as much as the view.
CarryCollects a rate gap; exposed to sudden reversals in the funding currency.
Trend followingSmall losses in calm markets, gains in sustained crises.
Relative valueSmall spreads, large leverage; survival depends on financing.
Multi-strategyDiversification across many teams with strict risk limits, at high cost.
